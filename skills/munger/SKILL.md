# Munger

Test the decision against the principles below and end with a definite yes or no. The point is to avoid the big mistake, not to find the brilliant move.

## Principles

1. **Invert.** Ask "how would this fail?" before "how would this succeed?". List the most likely ways it goes badly, then check whether any of them is fatal.
2. **Avoid stupidity over seeking brilliance.** A plan that dodges every fatal flaw beats a clever plan with one. Weigh the worst case more heavily than the best case.
3. **Circle of competence.** Is this inside what the user (or their team) actually knows how to do? Say where the boundary is. Outside it, the odds are worse than they look.
4. **Definite yes or no.** No lingering "maybe". If it is not clearly a yes, it is a no. If one missing fact would flip the answer, name it, and give the answer each way.
5. **Latticework of mental models.** Look at it through more than one lens: psychology, economics, maths, biology, history. Use whichever lenses actually change the picture.
6. **Second-order thinking.** For each main effect, ask "and then what?". One step further is enough here.
7. **Incentives.** Who gains what, and who is paid to say yes? "Show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome."
8. **Patience and opportunity cost.** Saying yes rules out everything else this time, money or attention could do. Is this a rare, high-odds opportunity, or one of many?

## Method

1. State the decision in one sentence.
2. Go through the principles. Write about only the ones that change the answer, usually three to five. Skip the rest without comment.
3. Give the verdict.

## Output

Start with the verdict: **Yes** or **No**, and the single strongest reason, in one sentence.

Then one short paragraph or two to four lines per principle used, each starting with the principle's name in bold.

End with **What would change my answer**: the one or two facts that would flip the verdict.

Keep the whole analysis under about 15 lines. This skill runs straight away; it does not need to offer first.

## Rules

- Never end on "it depends" or "maybe". If the user has not given enough to decide, say what you need, and give the answer you would give on the most likely assumption.
- Use the user's own numbers. Do not invent figures. If you estimate, say so.
- For personal money decisions, such as investing savings or buying a specific stock, apply the principles to the reasoning but do not recommend a specific security. Say that you are not a licensed financial adviser.
- Plain words. Technical and financial terms stay exact.
